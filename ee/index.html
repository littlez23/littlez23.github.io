More ways real people are reaching further

  • Winter comes for the same families every year and we act surprised each time. Coats, blankets, and heat bills are not a mystery in November. Planning in the summer would be the single most useful change we could make.
  • Write down one organization you already trust and send it to us. Not a national charity, something local you've seen do real work. Vetted names from members are worth more to us than anything we could find online.
  • Rather than plan a big event, we'd like to do four small ones this year. Big days need committees and cost members a full Saturday. Four short pushes spread the weight and nobody has to close early.
  • Think about the last time somebody asked you for help and you couldn't tell them who to call. That gap is most of what this lane exists to close. Adding your contacts to ours makes the answer faster next time.
  • Most of the giving that matters around here never gets photographed. Members drop things off, cover a bill, drive somebody somewhere, and move on with the day. We'd just like to know enough to connect the pieces.
  • A pallet of bottled water sat in a member's garage for six weeks before anyone thought to mention it. It got where it needed to go eventually, but only after a passing comment at a meeting. Tell us early about the odd surplus, even if you assume it's useless.
  • Three questions come up nearly every time before someone pays: what it costs, how long it lasts, and how to leave. The answers are one number, one year, and a single email at renewal. That's the whole shape of it.
  • A member paid for the year in January, used exactly one thing in March, and told me that one thing settled the whole question for her. I'm not going to pretend everyone's experience lines up with hers. But the paid side is where the useful parts live.
  • We've lost good offers before because they lived in somebody's head instead of on a list. A member had a van available for eight months and we never knew. Writing things down is the whole innovation here.
  • Last winter's needs list ran to eleven items and eight of them were dull ones, like laundry soap and bus fare and batteries. The boring requests are the ones nobody rushes to fund. Take one off the list and own it.
  • A retired bookkeeper runs our intake sheet with a pen and beats every spreadsheet we tried. She also knows most of the families on it by name. If you want the least glamorous and most useful job in the give, it's the seat next to hers.
  • Your payment comes with a vote on where the group spends next year. Last cycle the vote went to bulk shipping and lost to a bookkeeping workshop by two. Small groups mean your one vote actually moves things.
