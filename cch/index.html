More ways real people are reaching further

  • We used to be vague about the time commitment and it cost us good volunteers. So, plainly: a couple of evenings of training, then an hour or two a week. Free signup, and no obligation even after training.
  • There is a folder in our office labeled people who asked good questions. Nearly all of them started as an email address on a signup form. Ours is open right now and free.
  • Half the people on our list never take a match, and that is genuinely fine. Some write letters, some drive, some tell the right friend. Sign up and figure out later which one you are.
  • The list itself is unglamorous: a name, an email, a note about what you might want to do someday. No dues, no pledge form, no ranking. Some folks sit on it a year before they move.
  • Asking for help is not our strong suit, so here is the plain version. We keep a free list of people willing to be trained as mentors and advocates for kids in this county. Add yourself and we will take it from there.
  • Roughly four hours a month is what we ask of a mentor, and most people spend longer than that deciding. Put your name on the list while you think it over. Nobody here follows up with a sales call.
  • Three years is our average match length. That number took a decade of learning how to support volunteers instead of just recruiting them. Your money goes to the support side.
  • If you have five minutes, someone from our staff will walk you through exactly how a referral becomes a match. No slides, just a phone call. Most people who ask end up giving, but that is not the condition.
  • Kids notice who is getting paid. One of ours asked her mentor point blank about it, and the answer changed how she treated the whole relationship. Volunteers are free to the child and not free to run.
  • How many adults do you think a kid in foster care meets in a year who will still be reachable in five? Our answer to that question is the reason this organization exists. Funding pays for the answer to go up by one.
  • Mentors are not therapists and we do not claim otherwise. They are adults who show up, remember things, and ask questions in rooms where nobody else is asking. Training them to do that well is the expense.
  • Two hundred and some kids came through our program area last year and we could not match all of them. That gap is not a mystery to solve. It is a staffing and training cost we have not covered yet.
